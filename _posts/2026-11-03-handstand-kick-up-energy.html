Have you ever felt more frustrated learning to get up into a handstand than to stay in one? In this post we explain the energy cost of kicking up into a handstand.

You've trained hard: alignment, resistance, belly to the wall... and the first seconds of balance finally show up. Yeeee! But when it comes to the kick-up, you can't make it stick. It's frustrating, and it's hard to be present in the kick when all you're thinking about is balancing on hands.

The point is that the kick-up comes with a transition, and a transition is what happens when energy changes.

Consider the case of the start from a lunge with hands on the floor: we go from one stationary posture to another. Unlike the small oscillations in the handstand sways, here the legs have to travel a long way.

Setting up the energy

Model the body as an inverted pendulum pivoting at the wrists, with the centre of mass at distance \(h\) and \(\theta\) the lean from vertical. Choose the handstand as the zero of potential energy:

$$U(\theta)=mgh(\cos\theta-1)\le 0,\qquad K=\tfrac12 I\dot\theta^{2},\qquad E=K+U,\qquad I\approx mh^{2}$$

In the lunge we are at rest, so both potential and kinetic energy are zero: \(K=0\) and \(U_0=-mgh(1-\cos\theta_0)\).

The handstand requires still \(K=0\) and \(U=0\), so \(E=0\).

Our energy budget starts negative, and the muscles must pay the debt.

We assume that a small portion of the energy is dissipated along the way. As a result, the energy delivered by the muscles, minus the energy lost through inefficiencies and dissipation, must match the change in the system's mechanical energy:

$$W_{\text{muscles}}-W_{\text{lost}}=\Delta E\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; W_{\text{req}}=-U_0=mgh(1-\cos\theta_0)$$

With \(m=70\) kg, \(h=1\) m and \(\theta_0=55^\circ\), that is about 293 J, delivered in roughly 0.35 s (around 0.8 kW on average).

Note that in this case, muscular work is not limited to the wrists. The kick-up is a whole-body movement involving the coordinated action of the legs, hips, trunk, shoulders, and hands. The body behaves as an integrated system, with each segment contributing to the generation and transfer of mechanical energy.

Loading the leg stores energy in muscles and tendons. When the kick fires, the muscles do work at a rate \(P=\tau\dot\theta\) and the total energy rises:

$$\frac{dE}{dt}=\tau\dot\theta,\qquad I\ddot\theta=mgh\sin\theta+\tau$$

Once the kick ends, \(\tau\approx0\) and gravity takes over. The body decelerates as it rises, so \(K\) turns into \(U\) and moves back toward zero.

The final state is determined by the energy error \(\Delta E=E_{\text{final}}\):

$$\Delta E>0:\;\;\dot\theta_{\text{top}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta E}{I}}\;\;(\text{overbalance})$$ $$\qquad \Delta E<0:\;\;\theta_{\max}=\arccos\!\Big(1+\frac{\Delta E}{mgh}\Big)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2|\Delta E|}{mgh}}\;\;(\text{falls back})$$

Your hands can only absorb a small error, a degree or three of lean. In this model that is \(|\Delta E|\lesssim1.5\) J, about 0.5% of the 293 J. The kick lasts about 0.35 s, shorter than typical reaction times, so you can't correct it with feedback mid-flight. You have to get it right in advance.

Why holding is different

Near the top, the pendulum equation linearises to

$$\ddot\theta\approx\frac{g}{h}\,\theta\;\Rightarrow\;\theta(t)=\theta_0e^{\lambda t},\qquad \lambda=\sqrt{g/h}\approx3.1\ \text{s}^{-1}$$

A tilt doubles in about 0.2 s. That sounds fast, but the energy involved is tiny, so small finger and wrist corrections are enough.

In short, holding is a feedback task at almost zero energy, while the kick-up is a feedforward task that moves hundreds of joules and must land within about half a percent. Coordination matters because it controls how much energy the muscles deliver and when.

About the numbers: they come from a deliberately simple model (a single rigid pendulum, 70 kg, \(h=1\) m, starting at 55° from vertical). In a real body \(I\) and \(h\) change during the kick as hips and legs move, and the arms absorb part of the error. The logic is the same, but the exact figures are illustrative.
