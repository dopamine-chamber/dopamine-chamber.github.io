## Single inverted pendulum: a more accurate model for humans

The body tilting in a handstand is *more similar* to what in physics is known as **single inverted pendulum** (SIP), a pendulum where the center of mass above the pivot point. Due to its configuration, the system implements control logic to mantain the pivot point below the center-of mass and not falling, being so a common model used in biomechanics applications that include balance requirements like gait analysis, upright position and athletics skills, including handstands. In fact, (Figure 2).

Equations of motion becomes:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh\sin\theta+\tau = 0
$$

$$
mgh\sin\theta = - \tau 
$$

being θ the angle between the body axis and the vertical, suggesting that the magnitude of the torque generated by the wrist muscles depends on the tilt angle (Figure 3).

</figure>

This is reasonably consistent with what we observe in practice: small oscillations usually call for small adjustments that are not too demanding in terms of muscular effort. When the tilt becomes too large, the wrists can no longer counteract the moment generated by gravity, and we fall.

## You're not a simple harmonic oscillator

To introduce this topic, it is helpful to briefly revisit the simple pendulum and its equation of motion.

In a simple hanging pendulum (the one whose body is suspended from a fixed point that swings freely under gravity) the centre of mass describes **harmonic motion**, at least for small oscillations. In the ideal case, the angular position over time is modelled by a cosine function, $$\theta(t) = \theta_0 \cos(\omega_0 t)$$, where $$\theta_0$$ is the initial amplitude and $$\omega_0$$ is the angular frequency. 

I understand this may sound tedious and long-winded, but there is a reason why understanding the difference between the hanging pendulum model and the inverted pendulum model matters.

*And in case you didn't know, I love waves.*

Under this regime, its velocity continuously changes direction at the oscillation limits and varies in magnitude, reaching its maximum as the system crosses the vertical reference line.

This is what happens, more or less, to your center of mass when you use the control torque to recover balance. The common wisdom about controlling a handstand revolves, in fact, around this idea: if you are falling forward, push with your fingers; if you are falling backward, push with your palms. What you are being asked to do is behave like a **harmonic oscillator**.

Unfortunately for whose who genuinely approach handbalancing, as we have seen from the equation above, this result cannot be achieved without active control at the wrists ($$\tau$$).

Yet, an ideal inverted pendulum cannot be mathematically described by simple harmonic motion: it diverges exponentially from the vertical (for small angles). In other words, what we would like to obtain is simply not supported by the underlying physics of the system. 

This is due to the unfavorable configuration we occupy within the gravitational field: our pivot point is located at ground level, while the entire weight of our body is distributed above and rotates around it.

When balancing on hands, you (the inverted pendulum) are being asked to behave like a simple pendulum, turning an unstable system into one that oscillates about the vertical (our handstand). 

Furthermore, *even a real hanging pendulum does not behave like an ideal hanging pendulum*, and you are neither an hanging pendulum. Gravity is against you, and the system you most resemble is unstable by nature: left to itself, it tends to fall.

In short, in a hanging pendulum, harmonic motion (which is what we want our centre of mass to do around the vertical) is the natural behaviour of the system and needs no control, because gravity provides the restoring torque that brings it back to equilibrium. In an inverted pendulum, *gravity pushes us away from equilibrium*, so we have to *counteract it and recreate the harmonic oscillations ourselves*. 

Luckily, in reality, *you are not an hanging pendulum, nor a inverted one*, and you can train your muscle to describe what type of motion you want (differently from pendulums, they learn). So, once again: understand how the motion works, what its characteristics are, and how to reproduce it with our body.

With active wrist control, we can create behaviour that resembles simple harmonic motion. 

In other words, *nature will not produce restoring torques for you, but at least have provided you with the neuromotor control system which can learn how to generate them* (Figure 4).

In short terms, the ability to recover balance depends on the torque we can generate at the wrists. 

## If in doubt, be a pendulum

Either way, whether you are a hanging pendulum or an inverted one, being a pendulum is what truly matters: as we will see, this is the simplest configuration to study and define, and therefore the simplest case in which to predict and contain perturbations. Unfortunately, holding the posture that makes the body behave like a pendulum is very hard, and it involves non-trivial muscle activations. This is the reason why more experienced athletes, who have greater neuromuscular control, perform better in this specific task and are better able to maintain the position. As I said at the beginning of the post, the straight handstand was the hardest position for me to learn. Not to understand, but to actually learn. Things improved when I learned the correct **posterior pelvic tilt**. In the next posts we will look at what these activations are and how they affect the model. 

Moreover, because of the dependence of $$\tau$$ on $$h$$, this result also suggests that the magnitude of the torque required to recover balance depends on the height of the center of mass, meaning that taller people may struggle more in handstands (Figure 5).
