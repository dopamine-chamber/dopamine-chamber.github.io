At the macroscopic level, we can represent the human body as an articulated system: a collection of connected segments that can rotate relative to one another at the wrists, elbows, shoulders, hips, knees and ankles.

Let’s return to the straight handstand. Maintaining this position requires coordinated control of the body’s joint configuration, expressed through a beautiful alternation between flexion and extension:

- toes are plantar flexed
- ankles are plantar flexed
- knees are extended
- hips are extended
- thorax is flexed
- shoulders are flexed
- elbows are extended
- wrists are extended
- carpometacarpal (CMC) joints are flexed
- metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints are flexed

Note: The CMC and MCP joints are actually in an extended position. However, when weight is placed on the fingers, the muscles contract to push the floor away, generating active force. We will explore this mechanism in detail in a dedicated article.

Behind this mathematically fascinating symmetry, each joint contributes to the body’s overall mechanical behavior. Muscles generate forces and joint torques that help maintain the configuration, while the contact forces at the hands provide the external support needed to balance against gravity. When you change your shape in a handstand — for example, by opening your shoulders or arching your back — you change the positions of your body segments. This alters the distribution of mass relative to the joints and changes the torques required to control the movement.

In biomechanics and robotics, one way to study human movement is multibody dynamics, an approach that models the body as a series of rigid segments connected by joints. Instead of tracking every tissue deformation, we describe the configuration of each segment using a set of coordinates, such as joint angles (Figure 3).

Note that, even though we are still considering a straight handstand, this model is considerably more complicated than the inverted pendulum, and we need a more detailed mathematical description to capture what happens at each joint.

We can now use the equations of multibody dynamics to describe how those segments move and interact:

$$
\mathbf{M}(\theta)\ddot{\theta}+
\mathbf{C}(\theta,\dot{\theta})\dot{\theta} +
\mathbf{G}(\theta)=\boldsymbol{\tau} + \mathbf{J}_c^T\boldsymbol{\lambda}
$$

where:

<strong>θ, θ̇ and θ̈</strong> are, respectively, the joint coordinates, joint velocities, and joint accelerations.<br>

<strong>M(θ)</strong> is the mass matrix, which describes the system's inertia in its current configuration (in this case, the straight handstand).<br>

<strong>C(θ, θ̇)θ̇</strong> collects velocity-dependent dynamic effects, including Coriolis and centrifugal terms.<br>

<strong>G(θ)</strong> represents the effect of gravity.<br>

<strong>τ</strong> represents the generalized joint torques, including those generated by muscle activity.<br>

<strong>J<sub>c</sub><sup>T</sup>λ</strong> represents the reaction forces associated with constraints, such as the ground reaction forces acting on the hands.<br>

While the pendulum model is fragile when the handstand shape changes — as in the tuck or straddle positions, which introduce different levers whose projections lie in both the sagittal and frontal planes — the multibody model remains valid across the different positions introduced as practice advances. This matters when we add flexions or twists, or when other joints become pivots, for example when we bend the knees in a tuck position.
