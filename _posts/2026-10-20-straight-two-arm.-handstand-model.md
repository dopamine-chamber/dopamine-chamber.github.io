In this post we will use a first “simple” model to start understanding why handstands are intrinsically unstable. Let’s start with a simplified case: a straight, two-arm handstand. *Only the model is simple, because, to me, the straight handstand is anything but simple.*

<div style="text-align: left;">
  
- Rigid body: an idealisation in which the distance between any two points of the body stays constant: the body does not deform and can only translate and rotate. In biomechanics, the human body is often represented as a rigid-body model.<br>
- Newton’s second law: the net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration (ΣF = ma). Its rotational analogue is that the net moment equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration (ΣM = Iα).<br>
- Moment (of a force): the rotational effect of a force about a point. It is used interchangeably with torque in the post.<br>
- Angular velocity (ω): how fast the tilt angle changes over time [rad/s]. It is the time derivative of θ.<br>
- Angular acceleration (α): how fast the angular velocity changes over time [rad/s²].<br>
- Rotational dynamic equilibrium: the condition in which the net moment about the pivot is zero, so the angular acceleration is zero. The body is either still or rotating at constant speed.<br>
- Control torque (τ): the torque the athlete produces at the wrists to oppose gravity’s torque.<br
- Moment of inertia: a measure of a body’s resistance to changes in its rotation.<br>
- Centre of mass (CoM): the point where the whole mass of a body can be considered concentrated, and which moves like a single particle subject to the total external force.<br>
- Lever arm: the perpendicular distance between the line of action of a force and the axis of rotation.<br>
- Torque: the rotational effect of a force about an axis, equal to the force times its lever arm.<br
- Pivot: the fixed point about which the body rotates. In the model it is the wrists.
- Amplitude (θ₀): the maximum angle reached during the oscillation.<br>
- Angular frequency (ω₀): how fast the oscillation cycles [rad/s]. It is related to the period by T = 2π/ω₀.<br>
- Period (T): the time taken for one complete oscillation [s].<br>
- Hanging pendulum: a body free to rotate about a pivot, with its centre of mass below it. Gravity acts as a restoring torque, so the equilibrium is stable and, for small angles, the motion is harmonic.

## A first model: the body is a stick

Let’s start with a handful of assumptions: we suppose that the handstand system is perfectly symmetrical, with two arms pushing with the same force and in the same direction. We assume zero inclination in the frontal plane, and consider only the inclination that occurs in the sagittal plane when we push with both fingers and palms to adjust the balance. We also assume that arms, shoulders, hips and legs work identically and do not generate twists. If we initially treat the human body as a **single rigid body**, we can describe its motion through the position of its **centre of mass**. We are looking at the system from sagittal plane, that divides the encephalus and the rest of the body into right and left sections (Figure 1):


<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/single_segment_handstand_pendulum_model.png' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Single segment handstand">
  <figcaption>Figure 1. The body modelled as a single element described by its center of mass.</figcaption>
</figure>


In this configuration, that typically takes years of practice, the governing equation is Newton’s second law:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{CM}
$$

Taking moments about the pivot (the wrists), the rotational equation of motion is:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh+\tau
$$

where:

**I₀** = moment of inertia about the pivot (the wrists) [kg·m²]<br>
**α** = angular acceleration [rad/s²]<br>
**m** = body mass [kg]<br>
**g** = gravitational acceleration [m/s²]<br>
**h** = distance from the pivot to the center of mass [m]<br>
**τ** = control torque generated by the muscles [N·m]<br>
**θ** = angle between the body axis and the vertical [rad]

When we hold a handstand, the net external moment must be zero:

$$
mgh+\tau = 0
$$

meaning there are no accelerations. This condition defines the **rotational dynamic equilibrium**, satisfied if a body velocity is zero (stationary) or constant, and the moment generated by gravity is perfectly contrasted by the torque generated by the wrists:

$$
mgh = - \tau 
$$

In a real handstand, however, body is never perfectly stationary and perpendicular to the floor. In fact, small adjustments are performed at the hand level meaning the acceleration is not zero.

## Single inverted pendulum: a more accurate model for humans

A better model to represent the body tilting in a handstand is the **single inverted pendulum** (SIP), a common model used in biomechanics applications like gait analysis, upright position and athletics skills, including handstands. (Figure 2).

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/inverted_pendulum_handstand_model_r1.png' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Inverted pendulum model">
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Single segment inverted pendulum model for a straight two-arm handstand.</figcaption>
</figure>

with:

**θ** = angle between the body axis and the vertical [rad]

Equations of motion becomes:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh\sin\theta+\tau = 0
$$

$$
mgh\sin\theta = - \tau 
$$

suggesting that the magnitude of the torque generated by the wrist muscles depends on the tilt angle (Figure 3).

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/handstand_dynamic_equilibrium.gif' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Single inverted pendulum model">
  <figcaption>Figure 3. Motion of the inverted pendulum.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is reasonably consistent with what we observe in practice: small oscillations usually call for small adjustments that are not too demanding in terms of muscular effort. When the tilt becomes too large, the wrists can no longer counteract the moment generated by gravity, and we fall.

## The simple harmonic oscillator

In a hanging pendulum, the centre of mass describes **harmonic motion**, at least for small oscillations. In the ideal case, the angular position over time is modelled by a cosine function, $$\theta(t) = \theta_0 \cos(\omega_0 t)$$, where $$\theta_0$$ is the initial amplitude and $$\omega_0$$ is the angular frequency. I know this may sound tedious and long-winded, but there is a reason why understanding the difference between the hanging pendulum model and the inverted pendulum model matters.

*And in case you didn't know, I love waves.*

Under this regime, its velocity continuously changes direction at the oscillation limits and varies in magnitude, reaching its maximum as the system crosses the vertical reference line (Figure 4).

This is what happens, more or less, to your center of mass when you use the control torque to recover balance. The common wisdom about controlling a handstand revolves, in fact, around this idea: if you are falling forward, push with your fingers; if you are falling backward, push with your palms. What you are being asked to do is behave like a **harmonic oscillator**.

An inverted pendulum, on the other hand, diverges exponentially from the vertical (for small angles). This is where the **control torque** comes in. In other words, you are being asked to +behave like a harmonic oscillato+r: to turn an unstable system into one that oscillates about the vertical. The problem is that even a real hanging pendulum does not behave like an ideal harmonic oscillator, and you are neither an ideal pendulum nor a hanging one. The system you most resemble is unstable by nature: left to itself, it tends to fall.

In short, in a hanging pendulum, harmonic motion (which is what we want our centre of mass to do around the vertical) is the natural behaviour of the system and needs no control, because gravity provides the restoring torque that brings it back to equilibrium. In an inverted pendulum, *gravity pushes us away from equilibrium*, so we have to *counteract it and recreate the harmonic oscillations ourselves*. So, once again: understand how the motion works, what its characteristics are, and how to reproduce it with our body.

In other words, *the ability to recover balance depends on the torque we can generate at the wrists*. This is the reason why more experienced athletes, who have greater neuromuscular control, perform better in this specific task and are better able to maintain the position.

Either way, whether you are a hanging pendulum or an inverted one, being a pendulum is what matters: as we will see, this is the simplest configuration to study and define, and therefore the simplest case in which to predict and contain perturbations. Unfortunately, holding the posture that makes the body behave like a pendulum is very hard, and it involves non-trivial muscle activations. As I said at the beginning of the post, the straight handstand was the hardest position for me to learn. Not to understand, but to actually learn. Things improved when I learned the correct **posterior pelvic tilt**. In the next posts we will look at what these activations are and how they affect the model.

Moreover, because of the dependence of $$\tau$$ on $$h$$, this result also suggests that the magnitude of the torque required to recover balance depends on the height of the center of mass, meaning that taller people may struggle more in handstands (Figure 5).
