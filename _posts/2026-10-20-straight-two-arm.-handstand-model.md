In this post we will exploit a first simple model to start understanding why handstand are intrinsically unstable. Let’s start with a simplified case: a straight, two-arm handstand. 

## A first model: the body is a stick

Let’s suppose that the handstand system is perfectly symmetrical, with two arms pushing with the same force and in the same direction. We assume zero inclination in the frontal plane, and consider only the inclination that occurs in the sagittal plane when we push with both fingers and palms to adjust the balance. We also assume that arms, shoulders, hips and legs work identically and do not generate twists. If we initially treat the human body as a single rigid body, we can describe its motion through the position of its center of mass. We are looking at the system from sagittal plane, that divides the encephalus and the rest of the body into right and left sections (Figure 1):

The governing equation is Newton’s second law:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{CM}
$$

Taking moments about the pivot (the wrists), the rotational equation of motion is:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh+\tau
$$

where:

**I₀** = moment of inertia about the pivot (the wrists) [kg·m²]<br>
**α** = angular acceleration [rad/s²]<br>
**m** = body mass [kg]<br>
**g** = gravitational acceleration [m/s²]<br>
**h** = distance from the pivot to the center of mass [m]<br>
**τ** = control torque generated by the muscles [N·m]<br>
**θ** = angle between the body axis and the vertical [rad]

When we hold a handstand, the net external moment must be zero:

$$
mgh+\tau = 0
$$

meaning there are no accelerations. This condition defines the **rotational dynamic equilibrium**, satisfied if a body velocity is zero (stationary) or constant, and the moment generated by gravity is perfectly contrasted by the torque generated by the wrists:

$$
mgh = - \tau 
$$

In a real handstand, however, body is never perfectly stationary and perpendicular to the floor. In fact, small adjustments are performed at the hand level meaning the acceleration is not zero.

## Single inverted pendulum: a more accurate model for humans

A better model to represent the body tilting in a handstand is the **single inverted pendulum** (SIP), a common model used in biomechanics applications like gait analysis, upright position and athletics skills, including handstands. (Figure 2).

  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/inverted_pendulum_handstand_model_r1.png' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Inverted pendulum model">
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Single segment inverted pendulum model for a straight two-arm handstand.</figcaption>
</figure>

with:

**θ** = angle between the body axis and the vertical [rad]

Equations of motion becomes:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh\sin\theta+\tau = 0
$$

$$
mgh\sin\theta = - \tau 
$$

suggesting that the magnitude of the torque generated by the wrist muscles depends on the tilt angle (Figure 3).

## The simple harmonic oscillator

In our single inverted pendulum model, the center of mass describes a **simple harmonic oscillatory motion** along a circular arc, where the angular position over time is modeled by a cosine function, $$\theta(t) = \theta_0 \cos(\omega_0 t$)$$, where $$\theta_0$$ is the initial amplitude and $$\omega_0$$ is the angular frequency. Under this regime, its velocity continuously changes direction at the oscillation limits and varies in magnitude, reaching its maximum as the system crosses the vertical reference line (Figure 4).

Anyway, one way to interpret these results is that, when a handstand tilts, the ability to recover balance depends on the torque we can generate at the wrists.
More experienced athletes, who have greater neuromuscular control, perform better in this specific task and are better able to maintain the position.

However, in a real handstand, where micro-adjustments are performed by the fingers and palms to maintain balance, this motion deviates slightly from a pure harmonic profile. Transient accelerations and decelerations naturally occur due to neuromuscular control, but these variations must remain extremely small to stay within the stability limits of the system.

Moreover, because of the dependence of $$\tau$$ on $$h$$, this result also suggests that the magnitude of the torque required to recover balance depends on the position of the center of mass, meaning that taller people may struggle more in handstands. In the next posts, we will see that other parameters also play a role and that the wrists can exert only a limited torque, bounded by the size of the contact area under the hands: if $$mgh\sin\theta$$ exceeds that limit, the fall can no longer be stopped.
