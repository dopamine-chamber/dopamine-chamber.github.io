There is a simple physical truth behind the ability to hold a **handstand**: the constant, *inevitable* pull of gravity. In fact, beneath the apparent stillness, a vibrant orchestra of variables contribute to the balance. And to understand how they play their role in the symphony, we first need to capure the problem.

## Is the handstand a dilemma?

*Note: I am not a professional hand balancer, and the aim of this project is neither to provide a method for practicing handstands nor to teach how to perform them. Its purpose is to observe the phenomenon and describe the underlying principles that make it possible. What follows is a summary of the conceptual rabbit hole I have been exploring so far.*

For a long time, my engineering background led me to think of handbalancing as a problem of mechanical equilibrium. And for years, practicing handstands felt remarkably similar to solving a physics problem. 

*Which muscles should I recruit for a given task? (Which variables compose this system?)*<br>
*Which is the most important? (Which is the key variable?)*<br>
*How can I engage the strongest and most natural kinetic chain without introducing compensations? (Which method is the most effective?)*

And yet, I realize that rumours about how hard is to do a handstand are similar to what my Physics I professor said on the first day of the course:
"This is the first lecture, but physics is hard, so it's going to be a hard lecture."

Of course, the human body is literally not designed for handstands, nor for holding them for so long. Being upside down is not exactly what we evolved for. Anyway, I'm still fascinated by its ability to reorganize and adapt, even when faced with such an unusual, functionally useless, mentally draining and frustrating task. 

## Understanding models (understanding handstands?)

To undestand how this happens, we need a **model** that captures the problem. Therefore, over the years, my all-consuming side project has been refining this model to make it fit the handstand dilemma, and ultimately to find the perfect solution to the system. <br>
**And practicing handstands, of course**. 
     
Let's start with a short definition. A model is a simplified formalization of a real phenomenon or problem, expressed in a language that can be analyzed and that retains only the relevant variables. The first step is therefore choosing the level of abstraction: we need to determine which variables are relevant. Then we have to represent them in a language that allows precise treatment (e.g. equations).
On the other hand, models are valid *only within the domain they were built for*, and if reality departs from their assumptions, the model must be revised.

Therefore, if someone asked you: *“What is the best model for mathematically describing how to do a handstand?”*, the first answer would be: *“Which type of handstand?”*<br>
And even before that: *what actually is a handstand?* 

While we were able to give a clear and agreeable definition for the model, the same cannot be done for the handstand. Not only because there are many different handstand variations (even if this must be considered in the model, of course), but because it is hard to define the problem itself without without relying on personal experience.
*Handbalancing is deeply experiential, not spelled out. And complexity is part of the game.*

Therefore, modelling something that is hard to describe in explicit terms is no walk in the park. For me, it calls for an in-depth study.

From now on, trying to model the problem will happen on the three anatomical planes onto which human movement can be projected: the frontal (or coronal) plane, the transverse (or horizontal) plane, and the sagittal (or median) plane (Figure 1).

Armed with these hopes, we will analyse all the problems of the handstand and try to isolate them, study them one by one, and then combine them into a cocktail party that should be our handstand. We will use formulas, see with our own eyes the dependence on the parameters, and make some assumptions. And, as we know, answers will be valid only within the assumptions we chose. 

## A pinch of assumptions and a first model: the body is a stick

Let's start with a first extremely simplified model, but first we have to make our boring but necessary assumptions: we suppose that the handstand system is perfectly symmetrical, with two arms pushing with the same force and in the same direction. We assume zero inclination in the frontal plane, and consider only the inclination that occurs in the sagittal plane when we push with both fingers and palms to adjust the balance. We also assume that arms, shoulders, hips and legs work identically and do not generate twists. 

If we initially treat the human body as a **single rigid body**, we can describe its motion through the position of its **centre of mass**. We see its motion from the sagittal plane, that divides the encephalus and the rest of the body into right and left sections (Figure 1):


<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/single_segment_handstand_pendulum_model.png' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Single segment handstand">
  <figcaption>Figure 1. The body modelled as a single element described by its center of mass.</figcaption>
</figure>

In this configuration (that typically takes years of practice), the governing equation is Newton’s second law:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{CM}
$$

Taking moments about the pivot (the wrists), the rotational equation of motion is:

$$
\sum M_{O} = I_{O}\,\alpha = mgh+\tau
$$

When we hold a handstand, the net external moment must be zero:

$$
mgh+\tau = 0
$$

meaning there are no accelerations and the moment generated by gravity is perfectly contrasted by the torque generated by the wrists:

$$
mgh = - \tau 
$$

In a real handstand, however, body is never perfectly stationary and perpendicular to the floor. In fact, small adjustments are performed at the hand level meaning the acceleration is not zero. In the next post, we will complicate a bit this simple model to analyze the torques that keep it (barely) upright.
