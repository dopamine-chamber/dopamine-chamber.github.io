There is a simple physical truth behind the ability to hold a handstand: the constant, *inevitable* pull of gravity. In fact, beneath the appearent stillness, a vibrant orchestra of variables contribute to the balance. And to understand how they play their role in the symphony, we first need to capure the problem.

## Is the handstand a dilemma?

*Note: I am not a professional hand balancer, and the aim of this project is neither to provide a method for practicing handstands nor to teach how to perform them. Its purpose is to observe the phenomenon and describe the underlying principles that make it possible. What follows is a summary of the conceptual rabbit hole I have been exploring so far.*

For a long time, my engineering background led me to think of handbalancing as a problem of mechanical equilibrium. And for years, practicing a handstand felt remarkably similar to solving a physics problem. 

*Which muscles should I recruit for a given task? (Which variables compose this system?)*

*Which is the most important? (Which is the key variable?)*

*How can I engage the strongest and most natural kinetic chain without introducing compensations? (Which method is the most effective?)*

Human body is literally not designed for handstands, but I'm fascinated by its ability to reorganize and adapt, even when faced with such an unusual, functionally useless, mentally draining and frustrating task. To undestand how this happens, we need a model that captures the problem. Therefore, over the years, my all-consuming side project has been refining this model to make it fit the handstand dilemma, and ultimately to find the perfect solution to the system. And practicing handstands, of course. 

## Understanding models

Let's start with a short definition. A model is a simplified formalization of a real phenomenon or problem, expressed in a language that can be analyzed and that retains only the relevant variables. The first step is therefore choosing the level of abstraction: we need to determine which variables are relevant. Then we have to represent them in a language that allows precise treatment (e.g. equations).
On the other hand, models are valid only within the domain they were built for, and if reality departs from their assumptions, the model must be revised.

Applied to the reality of handbalancing, we can see that there are many different handstand shapes. Therefore, if someone asked you, “What is the best model for mathematically describing a person holding a handstand?”, the first answer would be: “Which type of handstand?”
Always start there. If you want to solve a problem, you first need to model it. When talking of handstands, complexity is part of the game. and it's everywhere.

Note: from now on, to model which torques act and where, we will consider the three anatomical planes onto which human movement can be projected: the frontal (or coronal) plane, the transverse (or horizontal) plane, and the sagittal (or median) plane (Figure 1).

And, as we know, answers will be valid only within the assumptions we chose.
